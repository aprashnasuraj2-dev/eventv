The subscription rules moved. The math did not.

This hub timestamps the regulatory boundaries because stale advice is dangerous. U.S. “click-to-cancel” articles from 2024 are especially easy to misread today; UK subscription-specific rules are also changing immediately after the 2026 holiday-shopping cycle.

US · CURRENT

2024 FTC amendment was vacated

The FTC’s March 2026 rulemaking record says the Eighth Circuit vacated the amended Negative Option Rule in July 2025 and the pre-2024 version was reinstated. Do not present the 2024 “click-to-cancel” amendment as current federal rule text.

US · CURRENT

Recurring-charge evidence still matters

FTC consumer guidance warns about free trials and auto-renewals; ROSCA and current FTC enforcement remain relevant to clear material terms, consent and recurring online charges. State law can add protections.

UK · 2026 BOUNDARY

New subscription regime starts later

The draft Consumer Subscription Contracts Regulations 2026 state a commencement date of January 1, 2027. Cyber Monday 2026 therefore must not be described using those future cooling-off/reminder rules as already active.

UK · CURRENT

Existing digital-content rule still matters

The 2013 Consumer Contracts Regulations remain the current 2026 baseline: a consumer can lose the cancellation right for non-tangible digital content once supply begins after express consent and acknowledgement.

EU

Consumer location drives VAT logic

EU OSS exists so cross-border sellers can account for VAT on consumer sales through one portal. Digital-content withdrawal can be lost after downloading/streaming starts when the consumer agreed to lose that right.

CA / AU / UK

Digital taxes are market-specific

Canada’s GST/HST for digital products depends on customer location/province; Australia applies GST rules to imported digital products/services and uses a non-resident registration threshold; UK digital services supplied to UK consumers are liable to UK VAT.

Historical rule trap: the FTC’s October 2024 press release described an amended “click-to-cancel” rule. That amendment was later vacated. This page uses the FTC’s 2026 record for current status, not the older announcement.

Decision model

Normalize four clocks before comparing a Cyber Monday subscription.

01Cash clock

When money actually leaves your card: today, monthly, annual upfront, after trial, or after a promotional term.

02Use clock

How many months you realistically expect to use the service. This can be far shorter than the prepaid term.

03Renewal clock

The first date and price at which the promotion ends or a new billing cycle begins.

04Cancellation clock

The practical deadline and method needed to stop the next charge, based on the seller’s terms and applicable law.

Counterexample: an annual plan at $96 has a lower monthly equivalent than a $10 monthly plan. But if you expect to use it only four months and the annual payment is non-refundable, the annual commitment costs $96 while four monthly payments cost $40. Lower monthly equivalent ≠ lower expected cost.

UK

HMRC says digital services supplied to UK consumers are liable to UK VAT; the current standard VAT rate is 20% for most goods/services. Product exceptions still exist.

EU

VAT on cross-border consumer sales is destination-oriented; OSS helps sellers report across member states. Rates differ by member state, so the tool does not hard-code one “EU rate.”

Canada

CRA says cross-border digital products/services can be subject to GST/HST based on the consumer’s usual place of residence and province; current federal/harmonized rates differ by province.

Australia

ATO says imported digital products/services sold to Australian consumers can attract GST; non-resident registration generally uses an A$75,000 turnover threshold. Use the checkout tax shown rather than assuming supplier status.

US

No single tax percentage is inserted here. Digital-service sales-tax treatment varies by state/local rules and service category; use the actual checkout amount for a purchase-level comparison.

Failure modes

Eight Cyber Monday comparison errors worth blocking.

  1. Monthly-equivalent illusion: annual upfront cash is presented as though it were month-to-month billing.
  2. Renewal omission: intro price is compared without the first post-promo price and term.
  3. Trial omission: “free” trial is recorded without the auto-charge date and cancellation path.
  4. Seat mismatch: one-seat monthly price is compared with multi-seat annual checkout.
  5. Tax mismatch: pretax vendor price is compared with tax-inclusive local price.
  6. FX omission: card conversion spread/fee is excluded from cross-border checkout cost.
  7. Policy invention: a lower Cyber Monday price is treated as a guaranteed price-adjustment right.
  8. Stale-law copy: the vacated U.S. 2024 rule or future UK 2027 regime is described as current Nov 2026 law.
