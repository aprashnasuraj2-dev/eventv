How to use the calculation responsibly

Suppose a group starts at an assumed 12:00 local time and a viewing point is assumed to be 40 km along the race route. At a constant 40 km/h the arithmetic gives 13:00; at 32 km/h it gives 13:15. The 13:00–13:15 window follows only from these invented inputs. It is not an official timetable or a forecast. A different start, climb, descent, delay or pace changes the result.

Route distance is distance actually ridden from the relevant start, not the straight-line distance from a city or the length of your journey to the roadside. An organiser’s map might show a neutral section, route changes or separate kilometre references. Check the exact reference before entering a number. The form rejects a distance beyond the published stage length, but cannot verify the location or whether a neutral section is included.

The tool uses the same local clock for the selected stage: UK local time for stages 1–3 and France local time for stages 4–9. It labels an after-midnight result as a later day; it does not convert travel between countries or time zones. The slow speed can generate an implausibly long window, making the limitations of your assumption visible. It rejects zero speeds and reversed speed ranges.

At 0 km both endpoints are the assumed start time. That does not establish when the race actually starts or when the first team departs. In a team time trial, teams have distinct starts, so a scenario for one team cannot stand for every competitor. For an actual plan, check the published race timetable and individual starts when available.

Choosing a stage and a viewing place

Stage 1 links Leeds and Manchester; stage 2 runs from Manchester to Sheffield. Stage 3 is a London team time trial. The organiser describes the first two as road stages through Yorkshire, the Pennines and surrounding areas, while London’s format takes teams through a city circuit. The type of experience, crowds, road crossings and suitable arrival time can differ substantially.

Following the race into France is a separate travel decision. Stage 4 starts at Le Touquet-Paris-Plage and ends in Calais, then the route moves south through several regions before the final mountain stages. A place named as a start or finish is not an instruction to stand at a specific street. Verify the detailed official route and the local public transport plan before booking or choosing a spot.

For a mountain location, a short-looking map distance can hide an uphill approach, limited parking or earlier vehicle restrictions. At any venue, consider shade, water, rain, toilets, access to medical support, and how you will return after roads reopen. Route crossing points may be restricted and race vehicles can precede and follow the riders. Follow official marshals and police instructions.

For step-free access, accessible toilets, viewing platforms or a companion’s needs, contact the relevant local organiser or authority using the official channels. An advertised town or a point on a map does not establish that a particular pavement, public transport stop or viewing area will be accessible on race day.

Before making travel commitments

  1. Recheck the organiser’s stage page for route revisions, stage start details, maps and published timetables. Keep the edition date with any downloaded itinerary.
  2. Check local authority and transport-operator notices for exact closure boundaries, pedestrian crossings, diversions, parking and reopening. These can change independently of the race’s headline route.
  3. For cross-Channel travel, check current carrier schedules and applicable passport, entry and documentation requirements with the responsible official authorities. A race itinerary is not travel permission.
  4. Book flexible arrangements where appropriate and allow time for crowds and unforeseen disruption. A modelled arrival window does not account for cancelled services or queue length.
  5. For the team time trial, check the actual start order and intervals once announced; a finish time for one team is not the end of all racing.
